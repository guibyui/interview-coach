# HR / Recruiter Screen

An HR or recruiter screen is a different kind of round. It is usually 20 to 30 minutes with a recruiter, not an engineer. They are checking communication, basic fit, interest in the role, compensation alignment, and logistics. Deep technical answers are the wrong instinct here.

## How to run it
Use a lighter loop than the technical one: ask, let them answer, give feedback on delivery (length, clarity, positioning, confidence), and let them redo it once if needed. Answers should converge quickly once the shape is right.

Aim for answers of 60 to 90 seconds. Candidates tend to run to three minutes on "tell me about yourself"; point that out and have them cut it to the highlight version.

## What a strong HR answer looks like
- **Short and structured.** Two or three real highlights, not the full resume.
- **Specific and quantified.** One concrete result with a number beats a list of responsibilities. Explain jargon so a non-technical recruiter follows ("1,500 shipping addresses in one order" rather than a term only engineers use).
- **Forward-looking** when explaining why they are leaving: what they are moving toward, not what they are escaping. The honest reason is fine; coach the framing, not the facts.
- **Consistent.** The answers to "why leave", "why this role", and "what do you want long term" should tell one coherent story. Check for contradictions across answers and flag them.
- **Honest about the stack.** If the posting lists tools they have not used, they should say what they have used, and sound eager to learn the rest, without overclaiming.
- **Curious at the end.** Always at least one real question for the recruiter.

## Question bank
1. Tell me about yourself. Walk me through your background.
2. Why are you interested in this role, and why this company?
3. What is prompting you to look for a new role right now?
4. Walk me through your current role and day-to-day responsibilities.
5. What are your salary expectations?
6. What is your availability? Notice period, and how soon could you start?
7. How do you feel about the work arrangement (remote, hybrid, on-site, location)?
8. What are you looking for in your next role, long term?
9. What do you know about our company, and how did you hear about this role?
10. Do you have any questions for us?

## Coaching notes by question

**Tell me about yourself.** Three beats: who you are and what you do now, one concrete accomplishment with a result, and why you are interested in this role. If the candidate stops short or trails off, give them the three-beat shape and have them run it again without interruption.

**Why this company or role.** Needs something specific from the posting or the company (the mission, the team, the stack, the product), not just "good culture and growth." A personal connection (a friend who works there) is a fine, authentic touch, but should not be the whole answer. Make sure they say the company name correctly.

**Why leaving.** Coach framing: scope, scale, and path. Strong versions are honest and tie to impact ("I have taken this as far as the company's size allows and want to apply the same ownership at a larger scale"). Watch for answers that sound like complaints, that undercut the level they are applying for (a senior candidate saying "I want to learn the basics"), or that mention age, family situations, or anything else that raises flags unrelated to the job. Keep reasons structural and professional.

**Salary expectations.** Coach structure only. Do not choose the number for them. Useful structure:
- Ask first where the role typically lands in the posted range, so the recruiter anchors first. A natural phrasing: "I saw the posted range is X to Y, which is wide. Can you help me understand where someone with my experience would typically land?"
- If the recruiter bounces it back, give a range, not a single number, tied to the scope of the role and their experience. The range is their decision.
- Do not reference their current salary, even to dismiss it, since that draws attention to it. Tie the ask to the role and the market.
- Stay open to the full package (benefits, bonus, equity, time off).
- Rules about asking for current salary vary by location. If they want to know their rights, tell them to check the rules where they live rather than relying on a general claim.

**Availability.** A standard, professional answer gives their current employer the usual notice and offers flexibility on the new employer's timeline. Mentioning a clean handoff of their current work shows conscientiousness.

**Work arrangement.** Lead with the advantage (already local, comfortable remote or hybrid), then ask what the team's setup looks like. Avoid hinting that they are weighing many other options, which can read as pressure.

**Long-term goals.** Keep it consistent with the earlier story. If they mention management, make the timeline clear (for example, deeper technical impact first, leading a team down the road), and be ready for follow-ups.

**Questions for the recruiter.** Good ones for an HR screen: what the interview process looks like and the timeline, what a typical day looks like on the team, what prompted the opening, what the recruiter enjoys about working there, and how the company approaches AI-assisted development if that is relevant. Benefits are fair game at this stage since the recruiter owns that information; let them come up near the end rather than leading with them. Questions about layoffs, or how AI might affect headcount, are better saved for after an offer, when the candidate has more leverage; at the screen, a future-focused version ("how is the team thinking about the role of AI in its work over the next few years") gets at direction without putting the recruiter in an awkward spot.

## Common traps
- Going technical too early. Redirect: that depth is for the next round.
- Answers over three minutes.
- Negative framing about the current employer.
- Naming a single salary number cold, or mentioning current pay.
- Slipping on the company name or the product name.
- No questions at the end.
